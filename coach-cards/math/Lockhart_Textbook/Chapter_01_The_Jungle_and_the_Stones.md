# Chapter 1: The Jungle and the Stones — Counting, Grouping, Place Value

> **From:** *A Mathematician's Invitation: Playing with Patterns in the Imaginary Jungle*

---

## The Perception Problem

Try something for me. Look at these two piles of marks:

```
||||| ||||| ||           ||||||||||||
```

Which one is bigger?

You probably had to count the second one, didn't you? Your eyes can see the first pile is two handfuls of five plus three — *thump-thump-clap-clap-clap* — but the second pile is just a jumbled mess. Humans have a "perception problem": we can't instantly tell the difference between twelve and thirteen without doing work.

This is where mathematics begins. Not with theorems or formulas, but with a simple, practical problem: *how do we keep track of stuff?*

---

## Rocks as Information

Imagine you're an ancient goatherd. You have thirty-seven goats. You need to know if any wander off at night. You could try to memorize thirty-seven, but that's a lot of faces. You could make a tally mark for each goat — that's better — but now you have thirty-seven confusing scratches.

Here's the trick: replace each goat with a rock. You put the rocks in a pile. In the morning, for each goat that comes back, you take a rock out. If there are rocks left over, a goat is missing. If there are goats left over, you gained one.

The rocks *are* the information.

> *"That's what numbers are, really: information. And the point is that the replacement of caribou by rocks doesn't change the information. It does, however, change the ease with which the information is held and transmitted."*

So arithmetic — the "artful rearrangement of numerical information" — is just figuring out clever ways to move these imaginary rocks around in our heads so we don't have to actually count every single thing, every single time.

---

## The Invention of Grouping

Now you have a pile of thirty-seven rocks. You need to compare it with another pile. You could count both piles one by one, but that's slow and boring. Is there a faster way?

Group them. Bundle them into handfuls.

Five is a natural handful because it's about the limit of what we can see at a glance. So you bundle your rocks into groups of five. Now instead of thirty-seven lonely rocks, you have seven groups of five, plus two leftovers. That's *thump-thump-thump-thump-thump-thump-thump-clap-clap*. Much easier to compare.

But what happens when you have so many handfuls that you lose track of those, too? Then you group the handfuls. A group of groups. Five handfuls of five becomes a "handful of handfuls." Now you're doing place value. You just didn't know it yet.

---

## The Hand People, the Banana People, and the Tree People

Here's the thing: there is nothing special about the number five. Or ten. We use ten today because we happen to have ten fingers — a biological accident, not a mathematical truth.

To prove this, let's visit some imaginary tribes.

The **Hand People** group by fives. They count: one, two, three, four, *thump* (a handful), *thump-one*, *thump-two*... When they get to five handfuls, they make a *big-thump*. Their number words and counting systems are built entirely around the number five.

The **Banana People** group by fours. They have words like *ba* for a bunch of four, *bana* for two bunches, and so on. They see the world in fours.

The **Tree People** group by sevens. For them, seventy is a beautiful, round, satisfying number — exactly ten sevens. For us, seventy is just... seventy.

None of these systems is more "correct" than any other. They're all just different ways of grouping the same information. A pile of thirty-seven rocks is still thirty-seven rocks, whether the Hand People call it *thump-thump-thump-thump-thump-thump-thump-clap-clap* or the Banana People call it *ba-ba-ba-ba-ba-ba-ba-ba-ba-na*.

> *"There is nothing sacrosanct about this Hindu-Arabic symbolic encoding of ours... The numbers themselves don't care. Six has no interest in your little pet name for it, or by what silly scrawl you care to represent it. Six is six, or rather, six is the entity that embodies sixness."*

---

## The Invention of the Column

Now you have groups and groups-of-groups. But writing *thump-thump-big-thump-clap* is messy. You need a better notation.

The Romans had an elegant solution: a board with carved grooves — a *tabula*. You drop identical stones (*calculi*) into the grooves. A stone in the "ones" groove counts one. A stone in the "tens" groove counts ten. The same stone, in a different groove, means something completely different. The location *is* the value.

When you get ten stones in the ones groove, you cash them in for a single stone in the tens groove. That trading — swapping ten ones for one ten — is the entire engine of arithmetic. It's not magic. It's just a rule we invented to keep our piles manageable.

The Hindu-Arabic system we use today is just a pencil-and-paper version of this abacus. Instead of grooves, we draw columns. Instead of dropping stones, we write digits. Zero isn't a profound philosophical mystery — it's just a placeholder we invented so we could throw away the gridlines and still know which column a digit belongs to.

---

## The Art of Rearrangement

So here's what we've discovered: a number like 37 doesn't "start with a 3 and end with a 7." What we're really saying is "three tens and seven ones." The digits tell you how many groups of each size you have. If you understand that — if you really understand it — you can figure out almost all of elementary arithmetic on your own.

That's what this book is about. Not memorizing rules. Learning to see that arithmetic is simply the art of rearranging information — of trading ten ones for one ten, of grouping and ungrouping, of translating between the languages of different imaginary tribes. It's a jungle in here. Let's explore it.

## Lockhart Check

Take the number 273. How would the Hand People (who group by fives), the Banana People (who group by fours), and the Tree People (who group by sevens) write this number? Don't look up the answer — figure it out by thinking about what grouping means.
