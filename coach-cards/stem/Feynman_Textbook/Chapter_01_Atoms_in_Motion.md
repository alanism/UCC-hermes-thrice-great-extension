# Chapter 1: The Jiggling of Things — Atoms in Motion

> **From:** *How to Think, Not What to Memorize: A Feynman-Style Guide to Understanding the Physical World*

---

## The Most Important Sentence

Let me ask you something. Imagine a disaster — a terrible cataclysm — wipes out every book, every computer, every library, every scientific paper. Everything. All knowledge is gone. But somehow, one sentence gets passed on to the next generation of creatures. Just one sentence. What would you pick?

I'll tell you what I'd pick. I believe the atomic hypothesis is the single most information-dense sentence in all of science:

> *All things are made of atoms — little particles that move around, are in perpetual motion, attract each other when they are some distance apart, but repel when squeezed into one another.*

That's it. If you understand that one sentence — really understand it — you can rebuild an enormous amount of physics and chemistry from scratch. You can figure out why water boils, why ice is hard, why air pushes on your skin, why a rubber band snaps back, why fire burns. It's all just atoms jiggling, attracting, and repelling.

Let's start with the jiggling.

---

## Heat Is Not What You Think It Is

You've been told your whole life that things are "hot" or "cold." That seems obvious. But what does "hot" actually mean at the level of the thing itself?

> *"Hot and cold is the speeds that the atoms are jiggling. If they jiggle more, it corresponds to hotter. Colder is jiggling less."*

Think about that. When you touch a hot stove, you're not feeling some mysterious fluid called "heat" flowing into your hand. You're feeling trillions upon trillions of atoms in the stove vibrating violently, banging into the atoms of your skin, making *them* jiggle faster too. That's what "burning" is — your atoms getting shaken awake.

What about cold? Same thing, reversed. Your atoms are jiggling fast, they touch atoms that are jiggling slowly, and your atoms slow down. That's all. There's no such thing as "cold" as a substance. Cold is just less jiggling.

Now here's a puzzle for you: if atoms have perfect elasticity — if they never lose energy when they bounce off each other — then how does a bouncing ball ever come to rest? The ball is made of atoms, right? Shouldn't it bounce forever?

The answer: the ball's atoms are jiggling, and they bump into the atoms of the floor, which bump into the atoms of the air, and all that organized up-and-down motion of the ball gets spread out into the random jiggling of billions of other atoms. The ball doesn't lose its energy — it *distributes* it. The floor gets ever so slightly warmer. The air gets ever so slightly warmer. The organized motion of the ball becomes the disorganized jiggling of everything around it.

---

## The Three Personalities of Matter

Now, why does the same stuff — water, for instance — sometimes act like a rock (ice), sometimes like a puddle (liquid), and sometimes like invisible air (steam)?

It's all about how hard the atoms are jiggling, and whether that jiggling is strong enough to break the grip they have on each other.

### Solid: The Crate of Oranges

In a solid, the atoms are packed together like oranges in a crate. They're all touching, held in a rigid pattern by the attraction between them. But they're not still. They're vibrating — jiggling in place — like a nervous crowd stuck in a packed subway car. The hotter the solid, the more violently they vibrate. But they can't break loose from their neighbors. They're trapped.

### Liquid: The Breaking Point

Now turn up the heat. The atoms jiggle harder and harder. Eventually, some of them jiggle hard enough to break the grip of their neighbors and start sliding past each other. That's melting. The rigid crate pattern breaks down, and the atoms roll and tumble over one another. But they're still close — they still feel each other's attraction, pulling them into a droplet.

Why does water form round drops? Because the atoms on the surface have neighbors only on one side — pulling them inward. The surface gets stretched tight, like a tiny rubber balloon. That's surface tension.

### Gas: The Escape

Now turn the heat up even more. Some atoms at the surface jiggle so violently — they get hit extra hard by their neighbors — that they fly completely free. They escape the liquid entirely and go bouncing around all by themselves. That's evaporation. Keep doing that to all of them, and you get steam — a gas.

Here's the beautiful part: the atoms that escape are the ones that got hit *harder than average*. Which means the atoms left behind are moving slower than before. Their average jiggle speed drops. And what do we call that? Cooling. That's why blowing across a bowl of hot soup cools it down — you're helping the fastest-jiggling water atoms escape, taking their energy with them.

---

## The Invisible Battering Ram

So what does gas pressure feel like? Imagine a room filled with thousands of ping-pong balls, all bouncing around wildly, bouncing off the walls, off the ceiling, off your face. That's gas. Every time you feel wind on your skin, you're feeling trillions of gas atoms smashing into you.

Now, if we squeeze twice as many atoms into the same space — or make them jiggle twice as fast (heat them up) — they hit the walls twice as often. Pressure goes up. That's how a steam engine works. That's how a car engine works. That's how a firecracker works. You give atoms more room to jiggle or make them jiggle harder, and they push on everything around them.

## The Only Question That Matters

Let me leave you with this. Look around wherever you are. Look at a table, a cup, your own hand.

> *"The world is a dynamic mess of jiggling things if you look at it right. And if you magnify, you will hardly see a little thing anymore because everything is jiggling in its own pattern, and there's a lot of little balls."*

Everything you see is a pattern of bouncing, vibrating, attracting, repelling atoms. The difference between a rock, a flame, a cloud, and your brain is — at the deepest level — just the arrangement and jiggling speed of these little particles.

That's it. That's the first chapter.
